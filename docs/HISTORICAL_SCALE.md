# Historical Scale

This is the scaffold for a history spanning millennia. It is deliberately a hierarchy, not a premature date-by-date chronology.

## Timeline hierarchy

```text
Civilizational history
└── Era (large historical transformation)
    └── Age (a recognizable social / technical configuration)
        └── Period (regional or institutional change)
            └── Event cluster (linked decisions and consequences)
                └── Event (a claim with provenance and confidence)
```

Each layer should answer a different question:

- **Era:** What changed about humanity's overall condition?
- **Age:** What kind of society became normal?
- **Period:** Which institutions, regions, or populations changed?
- **Event cluster:** How did a chain of causes produce a turning point?
- **Event:** What happened, according to which evidence, and how certain are we?

## Provisional era sequence

These are labels for organizing research and invention, not final in-world names or dates.

| Order | Working era | Core question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Fracture | How did an election crisis, a false attribution, and nuclear attacks break the existing order? |
| 2 | The Reckoning | What happened during the violent global upheaval, and how did survivors preserve truth and dignity? |
| 3 | The Repair | How were basic needs, trust, accountability, and public institutions rebuilt? |
| 4 | The Enabling Age | How did technology become broadly empowering rather than controlling? |

## Required record for a major event

When an event is promoted from an idea into the chronology, capture:

- **Event ID:** stable identifier that does not depend on its title.
- **Time range:** exact date if known; otherwise an interval or relative placement.
- **Location / scope:** planet, system, region, civilization-wide, or unknown.
- **Claim:** concise description of what happened.
- **Causes:** contributing conditions and choices.
- **Consequences:** immediate and long-term effects.
- **Participants:** individuals, communities, institutions, and populations affected.
- **Evidence:** source records, testimony, artifacts, simulations, or in-world documents.
- **Confidence:** confirmed, strongly supported, disputed, speculative, or legendary.
- **Perspective:** who is telling the account and what they could know.
- **Canon state:** proposed, accepted, superseded, or rejected.
- **Continuity links:** earlier causes and later consequences.

## The three-history rule

For any consequential event, keep these distinct:

1. **What happened:** the current best-supported reconstruction.
2. **What survived:** evidence available to later people.
3. **What people believe happened:** local traditions, political narratives, education, myths, and deliberate misinformation.

These can disagree. The disagreement should be intentional and traceable, not an accidental continuity error.

## Scale without false precision

Do not assign exact years to remote eras until the story needs them. Use ranges and relative order while the structure is fluid. Avoid pretending a civilization spanning many star systems has a single universal calendar unless the setting establishes how that calendar is maintained and why people use it.

The historical hierarchy should eventually be navigable in both directions: from a millennial era down to a local incident, and from a campaign event back up to the civilizational forces that made it possible.
