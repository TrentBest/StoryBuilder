# Story Foundation

> Working premise, not immutable canon. This document records the creator's initial intent so later worldbuilding can expand it without losing its roots.

## The promise

The story begins in a future so distant that humanity has become a civilization of worlds rather than a civilization with a home planet. There are more inhabited places, settlements, habitats, stations, and frontier communities than any single book could reasonably list. Earth is remembered, but its location is unknown to the people living in the era of the main story.

The audience should feel that the universe extends beyond the current narrative. A character may know a route, a world, a local history, or a rumor; no character needs to know everything.

A guiding line captures the tone:

> "I could tell you all the worlds, but that would take us forever..."

This is not a substitute for worldbuilding. It is a promise that the world is larger than the page.

## Tonal DNA

The setting combines:

- **Civilizational consequence:** institutions, economics, logistics, technology, ideology, and historical memory have material effects.
- **Lost-homeworld mystery:** Earth is known through incomplete records, cultural inheritance, disputed accounts, and the occasional hard clue.
- **Frontier intimacy:** remote communities make the vast setting personal through work, family, loyalty, conflict, humor, and survival.
- **Earned hope:** peace and enabling technology are achievements built and maintained by people, not magical outcomes.
- **Moral complexity without a required dark lord:** no immortal emperor, chosen ruler, or single personality is necessary to hold the setting together.

Influences are reference points, not canon to copy. The setting must develop its own institutions, history, language, aesthetics, and conflicts.

## Historical spine: a speculative alternate future

The earliest period begins recognizably close to our own era. A fictional authoritarian leader rejects an election result. The crisis escalates into a nuclear attack on San Diego and Los Angeles, falsely attributed to Iran. The deception and the attack shatter trust, destabilize alliances, and help precipitate a global breakdown.

This is fictional alternate history, not a prediction or a claim about real events. The leader will not be named directly in the narrative. The intended recognition comes from a cumulative pattern of public behavior, rhetoric, institutional conflict, and choices—not from a single label. The narrative should make the allusion legible while allowing the fictional person and chronology to stand on their own.

The catastrophe is followed by a global reckoning of extraordinary severity—one whose cruelty makes even the remembered fall of old dynasties seem restrained. Its details must be developed carefully and with human consequences in view. Mass violence is a historical horror, not a spectacle to celebrate.

From that rupture, humanity gradually builds a more peaceful global order. With predatory concentrations of wealth and political power no longer able to capture the future in the same way, technology increasingly serves as an enabler of human agency rather than a mechanism of confinement. This change is neither instant nor perfect: it has institutions, safeguards, disputes, costs, and people who must keep choosing to maintain it.

Humanity then expands outward in stages:
1. Recovery and a new planetary settlement.
2. A durable, cooperative Earth-centered civilization.
3. Permanent settlement throughout the Solar System.
4. Interstellar migration and the founding of another star system.
5. Repeated waves of expansion, divergence, reconnection, and new settlement.
6. A civilization so distributed that no single catalog can describe all its worlds.
7. The era in which the main story begins: Earth is part of humanity's deep past, but its location is unknown.

## Design rules

1. **Scale by layers.** Establish eras before centuries, centuries before decades, and local histories only when the story needs them.
2. **Keep causes visible.** Major changes must have pressures, actors, choices, and consequences; do not jump from catastrophe to utopia in one paragraph.
3. **Separate fact from memory.** The actual past, the surviving evidence, and what later cultures believe about it are three different things.
4. **Make the future livable.** Show ordinary work, maintenance, relationships, local governance, trade, and play alongside grand events.
5. **Do not enumerate the universe prematurely.** Create places when they serve history, character, travel, or the campaign. Track totals and relationships without pretending the current list is complete.
6. **Technology should enable agency.** New capabilities can introduce new problems, but the default question is how people use technology to become more capable—not how every invention becomes a cage.
7. **Peace requires upkeep.** A peaceful civilization needs credible ways to resolve conflict, distribute resources, correct abuses, and handle disagreement.
8. **Keep the creator's intent distinct from canon.** A proposal is not canon merely because it appears in a draft.

## Open questions—not yet canon

- What exactly constituted the global reckoning, and who was harmed, protected, displaced, or held accountable?
- What institutions replaced the failed political and economic arrangements?
- What technical and social breakthroughs made peaceful cooperation durable?
- How did humanity cross the gap from Solar System settlement to interstellar settlement?
- What caused Earth to become unlocatable: natural change, deliberate secrecy, broken navigation infrastructure, political choice, or a combination?
- Which records survived, and which later cultures have reasons to distort them?
- What does a frontier community in the main era need, fear, trade, and celebrate?

## Canon status

Everything in this file is **foundational working intent**. Individual events, dates, mechanisms, and institutions become canon only when deliberately accepted in the project record. Unresolved details remain open rather than being silently filled in.
